*Teenagers taking more candy than they should is selfish. Posting their identifiable faces across neighborhood social media is still a disproportionate response. If the lesson is supposed to be community responsibility, adults have to model it too.*

**November 1, 2026 | Blackthorn Field Notes**

Every November 1, neighborhood social media seems to host the same argument.

Someone left a bowl of candy outside. A group of teenagers took more than the handwritten sign allowed, sometimes emptying the bowl. A doorbell camera recorded them. By morning, adults were posting identifiable pictures across Facebook, Nextdoor, and community groups with some version of the question: “Are these your kids?”

The teenagers behaved selfishly. They took something offered to a community and ignored the expectation that they leave enough for other children. I am not defending that choice, and I am not going to turn it into a charming story about youthful mischief simply because the stakes were small.

But the stakes were small.

That matters when deciding what a proportionate response looks like.

A child taking too much free candy does not become public property. A teenager making an inconsiderate decision does not give every adult in the neighborhood an ethical right to distribute their face, speculate about their character, invite strangers to identify them, and preserve the incident in screenshots that may travel farther than anyone intended.

The behavior can be wrong. The response can still be worse.

## An honor system includes predictable risk

When I leave a bowl of candy outside, I understand what I am doing. I am choosing convenience and trust. I am inviting people to take something without direct supervision, and I know someone may take more than I wanted them to.

That does not make taking everything considerate. It does mean the possible loss was built into the arrangement. The candy was placed outside to be taken for free, under an informal limit that depended entirely on people choosing to honor it. Sometimes people will not.

Adults understand this risk when we set up the bowl. We may be disappointed, annoyed, or angry when someone abuses the offer. We can talk about why the behavior is selfish. We can decide to hand out candy in person next year, place out smaller batches, or stop participating entirely.

What we should not do is act as though a teenager taking extra candy created a public emergency requiring mass identification.

Even if someone wants to use the word theft, the label does not settle the question of proportion. We do not become more responsible by choosing the largest audience available for the smallest act of accountability.

## A camera does not make every recording a public notice

Doorbell cameras give ordinary people a power that used to belong mostly to institutions and news organizations: the ability to record strangers, select a frame, attach a story, and distribute it instantly to an audience of hundreds or thousands.

That power feels ordinary because the technology is ordinary. The camera is already there. The app makes sharing effortless. The neighborhood group is full of people who live nearby. Posting can feel like the natural next step after reviewing the footage.

It is still a publication decision.

The image has a subject. The caption creates a frame. The comments create a public record of speculation. Someone identifies a school, a family, an address, or a name. Someone saves the image. Someone reposts it without the original explanation. The child loses control over both the audience and the meaning attached to their face.

UNICEF’s 2026 report on children’s best interests in digital policy found that children repeatedly raised concerns about adults or peers taking screenshots, sharing photos without consent, exposing private information, and using images in ways they could no longer control. Younger children specifically described being photographed in public and posted online without permission as a source of fear and powerlessness. [Read the UNICEF report](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/media/12841/file/UNICEF-Innocenti-Children%27s-Best-Interests-Digital-Policy-Practice-report-2026.pdf).

The details are different here. The teenagers were recorded in a public-facing space, and the images were connected to conduct adults disliked. But the underlying power imbalance is the same. Adults control the camera, the account, the caption, the audience, and the duration. The child controls almost none of it.

## Accountability is supposed to repair something

We use the word accountability as though it means making someone feel bad enough.

It does not.

The U.S. Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention describes restorative accountability as taking responsibility for behavior and taking action to repair harm. That includes understanding who was affected, acknowledging the choice, making amends where possible, and changing the behavior. Punishment alone does not necessarily accomplish those goals. [Read the federal restorative-accountability guidance](https://ojjdp.ojp.gov/sites/ojjdp/files/pubs/implementing/accountability.html).

That distinction is useful far beyond the justice system.

If a teenager emptied a candy bowl, what repair is actually needed? An apology could matter. Replacing the candy could matter. A conversation about why the honor system depends on reciprocity could matter. A parent learning what happened and helping their child make it right could matter.

Thousands of strangers seeing the child’s face does not replace the candy. It does not restore the experience for the younger children who arrived later. It does not reliably teach empathy. It teaches that mistakes become content and that adults respond to a small abuse of trust by using a much larger power without restraint.

Humiliation may produce fear. Fear is not the same as understanding.

## “I just wanted the parents to know” is not enough

Some people posting these images are not trying to ruin a child’s life. They may genuinely want the parents to see what happened. They may believe the quickest route to a private conversation is a public post asking whether anyone recognizes the teenagers.

Intent matters, but it does not control impact.

A post addressed to parents is still visible to classmates, employers, extended family, neighbors, strangers, and anyone who receives a screenshot. The comments rarely remain limited to identification. Adults begin assigning character: thief, delinquent, future criminal, bad kid, evidence of failed parenting. A few minutes of behavior becomes a referendum on an entire child and family.

The U.S. Surgeon General’s social-media advisory warns that young people’s online experiences can affect mental health and specifically urges people not to participate in or redistribute online harassment and abuse. It also recognizes that online content can be stored and shared beyond its original audience. [Read the Surgeon General’s advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/youth-mental-health/social-media/index.html).

I am not claiming every critical neighborhood post meets a clinical or legal definition of cyberbullying. I am saying adults should recognize the same features that make online humiliation dangerous: a power imbalance, rapid amplification, loss of context, permanent copies, and an audience the subject cannot meaningfully control.

If the goal is truly to reach a parent, the method should minimize harm while accomplishing that goal. Public exposure should not be treated as the default search function for someone else’s child.

## Children are supposed to have room to become better

Teenagers are not toddlers. They know that taking an entire bowl is unfair. They are capable of responsibility, apology, and repair.

They are also still developing judgment, impulse control, identity, and an understanding of consequences. That does not excuse every decision. It is one reason our response should leave room for learning rather than freezing the worst available frame of a child into a permanent public identity.

Adults often say children need consequences. I agree. A consequence should connect to the behavior and help prevent it from happening again. Public shame is often disconnected from both goals. It reaches far beyond the people affected, continues long after the event, and gives the child no meaningful way to complete the consequence.

How much humiliation is enough? How many views make the lesson complete? When does the child get to stop being the kid from the Halloween post?

Digital punishment has no natural ending. That alone should make adults cautious.

## Proportionate responses exist

There are better options, even when the behavior is frustrating.

If you recognize the children or know their parents, contact the family privately. Describe what happened without enlarging it into a judgment about the child’s entire character. Ask for replacement candy, an apology, or a conversation about the younger children who were affected.

If you do not know them and want to alert the neighborhood, post a nonidentifying description of the incident. Blur faces, clothing logos, house numbers, license plates, school identifiers, and other details that make identification easy. Talk about the behavior and the community expectation without turning the children into a public exhibit.

If the footage shows an actual safety threat, violence, property damage, or conduct that reasonably requires official intervention, preserve the original evidence and provide it directly to the appropriate authority. Evidence can be useful without becoming entertainment.

And if the harm is simply that the bowl was emptied, it is reasonable to be angry and still decide that the event does not require a hunt. Sometimes the proportionate response to a small violation is a small response.

Restraint is not permission. It is judgment.

## Adults are part of the lesson

An honor system asks people to consider someone besides themselves. Take what is fair. Leave enough for others. Respect the generosity that made the shared resource possible.

Those are good values.

They apply to adults too.

When we possess footage that could embarrass a child, we are also being trusted with something. We can use it narrowly to address the behavior, or we can take more power than the situation requires because the platform made that power available.

If we choose the second option, we are repeating the same moral failure we claim to be correcting. We are taking far more than we need and leaving the consequences for someone else.

The teenagers should have left candy for the next child.

The adults should leave room for the teenagers to grow beyond one selfish decision.

Accountability matters. Privacy matters. Proportion matters. The dignity of a child does not disappear because a camera captured them doing something wrong.

If the lesson is supposed to be community responsibility, then adults have to model it.

We do not teach children to respect other people by forgetting to respect them.
