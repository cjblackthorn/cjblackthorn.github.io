Fine. Not all men caused my pain. But more than enough have, and I am exhausted by the expectation that I must carefully protect men's feelings whenever I speak about it. I have been treated like a body instead of a person, an object of sexual gratification, a hole to be used, or someone whose value depends on what I can provide. I have experienced entitlement, objectification, and disregard for my boundaries often enough that I am tired of pretending each encounter exists in isolation. When I finally said how much that treatment has hurt me, some of the responses focused not on the harm, but on reminding me that not every man behaves that way.

Then a cisgender woman responded with a question: "Aren't you a biological man?" In two short reactions, I was confronted with two versions of the same refusal to listen. First, I was expected to soften my experience so men who considered themselves innocent would not feel accused. Then I was expected to defend my womanhood before my experience could be taken seriously at all. One response tried to protect the reputation of the group I was criticizing. The other tried to place me inside that group to disqualify my perspective. Neither dealt with what I had actually said. Both made someone else's discomfort more important than the harm I was describing.

## The Disclaimer Is the Diversion

Of course it is not all men. I know that. We all know that. Women are perfectly capable of distinguishing individuals from groups, and we do not need a lesson in the fact that human behavior is not universal. There are thoughtful, compassionate, respectful men who understand consent, honor boundaries, and treat women as complete human beings. I have known men like that, and I have never struggled to acknowledge their existence. What I struggle to understand is why that acknowledgment must be repeated before a woman is allowed to speak honestly about the men who have hurt her.

When the response to a repeated pattern of mistreatment is "not all men," the conversation shifts. Instead of asking what happened, how often it happens, or why the behavior is so routinely excused, we begin discussing whether the person describing it has been sufficiently fair to people who were not involved. The woman telling the story becomes responsible for managing the feelings of listeners who feel implicated. She is asked to clarify her words, narrow her criticism, offer reassurance, and prove that she is not making a sweeping accusation. The original harm remains unexamined while the person who was hurt is assigned more emotional work.

Recognizing a pattern is not the same thing as assigning collective guilt. I am not saying every man is dangerous, abusive, or incapable of compassion. I am saying that enough men have treated me in ways that share recognizable features, and I am asking why those behaviors are still so familiar. We should be able to examine entitlement, objectification, and the social permission people give one another without pretending that every observation about a pattern is an indictment of every person within a category. If you hear a woman describing her pain and your immediate priority is defending the reputation of men, it is worth asking why that reputation feels more urgent than what happened to her.

## Being Different Is Something You Do

I do not need a man to announce that he is different. I need him to demonstrate it, particularly when doing so is uncomfortable. It is easy to be respectful when you are speaking to a woman whose approval you want, and it is easy to condemn coercion or cruelty when everyone around you already agrees. The more meaningful test comes when a friend reduces a woman to her body, treats a rejection like an invitation to negotiate, or makes a degrading comment because he assumes the room will tolerate it. Do you challenge that behavior when you safely can, or do you quietly distance yourself from it while leaving the person responsible completely unchallenged?

I am not asking men to carry personal blame for every other man's actions, nor am I asking anyone to put themselves in danger to prove a point. I am asking for something more ordinary and more consequential: refuse to participate, stop rewarding the behavior, support the person being targeted, and confront it when you have the opportunity and it is safe to do so. There is a difference between not committing harm yourself and helping create an environment where that harm is less likely to be dismissed. The latter may require social courage, and it may not earn applause. That is precisely why it matters.

If you are not one of the men I am describing, you do not need to interrupt my account to announce your exemption. You can listen, acknowledge the pattern without accepting responsibility for conduct that was not yours, and let your choices speak for you. Being decent is not an identity you declare and then expect women to validate. It is a practice that becomes visible in how you treat people and what you are willing to challenge. **Do not tell me you are safe. Help make the room safer.**

## When My Womanhood Becomes Inconvenient

The question about whether I am a "biological man" was not an argument about the behavior I had described. It offered no insight into entitlement, objectification, coercion, or boundaries. It was an attempt to change the subject by changing who I was supposedly allowed to be. Rather than engage with my experience, the commenter made my gender the controversy. Suddenly, it was not enough for me to describe what had happened; I was expected to establish my right to describe it as a woman. That is not a rebuttal. It is a way of denying someone standing in the conversation.

As a transgender woman, I recognize a particularly ugly contradiction in this. Trans women can be desired, pursued, fetishized, and reduced to our bodies by people who will not publicly respect our womanhood. Someone may be comfortable treating a trans woman as a sexual possibility while treating her identity as a subject for debate. She can be woman enough to attract attention, but apparently not woman enough to speak about what that attention has cost her. I am not claiming every person who desires a transgender woman behaves this way. I am describing the cruelty of being wanted as a body while being rejected as a person, and the way that cruelty becomes especially clear when our dignity is treated as conditional.

What made the comment sting was how precisely it repeated the problem I was already naming. I was speaking about people who reduce women to bodies and disregard their humanity. The response was to reduce me to a supposed biological classification and suggest that my experience therefore did not count. That is a particularly revealing failure of solidarity. If your concern for women disappears when the woman speaking is transgender, you are not defending women as a whole. You are drawing a boundary around whose pain deserves recognition and appointing yourself the person who gets to enforce it.

## Misogyny Is Not a Chromosome Test

Misogyny is not explained by a chromosome chart, and respect is not guaranteed by someone's gender. Misogyny is expressed through beliefs, social expectations, power, and behavior. Cisgender men can perpetuate it. Transgender men can perpetuate it. Cisgender women can reinforce it, including by using transphobia against other women. Transgender women can experience misogyny and can also be accountable for their own harmful conduct. No identity makes a person automatically compassionate, and no argument about anatomy makes someone else's mistreatment disappear.

Reducing this discussion to biology does more than miss the point. It risks excusing conduct by treating it as inevitable, as though entitlement and cruelty are simply what certain bodies do. I reject that. People learn patterns of behavior, people make choices, and communities decide what they will challenge or normalize. The fact that these patterns are social does not make their consequences imaginary. It means change is possible, and that accountability is meaningful precisely because people are capable of behaving differently.

Nor does recognizing what women experience erase the suffering of men. Men can be victims of abuse, exploitation, and sexual violence, and they deserve support without qualification. The [CDC's 2023/2024 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey](https://www.cdc.gov/nisvs/media/pdfs/sexualviolence-brief.pdf) estimated lifetime contact sexual violence among 45.1% of U.S. women and 16.9% of U.S. men. Those figures are about specific forms of sexual violence, not every kind of objectification or mistreatment I have described, but they make clear that these harms deserve serious attention. The [2022 U.S. Transgender Survey](https://ustranssurvey.org/report/safety/) also found that 39% of its respondents reported online harassment because of their gender identity or expression in the previous year. There is room to recognize all of these experiences without turning compassion into a contest or demanding that one person's story account for everyone else's before it can be heard.

## Solidarity Is Not a Label You Give Yourself

The two responses that prompted this essay were different, but they accomplished something remarkably similar. "Not all men" shifted the conversation toward reassuring a group whose reputation felt threatened. The question about my biology shifted it toward determining whether I counted as a woman in the first place. Both avoided confronting the mistreatment itself. Both placed an additional burden on the person who had spoken about it. Instead of being heard, I was being asked to defend the wording of my pain and then the legitimacy of the person feeling it. That is not listening, and it is not a path toward accountability.

Real allyship is often less dramatic than people imagine. It means listening without making yourself the center of a disclosure, considering criticism without immediately looking for an exemption, and being willing to challenge harmful conduct when doing so has a social cost. It also means understanding that disagreement is not permission to strip someone of their dignity. I do not expect everyone to agree with every word I write. I can be frustrated, imperfect, or mistaken, and my arguments should be open to thoughtful criticism. But there is a difference between questioning an argument and using someone's identity to dismiss her before the argument has even been addressed.

I want a world in which women can name patterns without being accused of hating every man, where men who have been harmed can speak without being dismissed, and where transgender people are not forced to pass someone else's test of legitimacy before receiving basic empathy. I want relationships in which desire does not become entitlement, boundaries do not become negotiations, and being useful is not mistaken for being valued. These are not demands for special treatment. They are expectations of ordinary human dignity, and they require us to care more about what our behavior does to people than about what we would prefer to call ourselves.

## The Point Was to Make the Harm Stop

I am tired of being expected to describe what has happened to me in language carefully engineered not to inconvenience anyone else. I am tired of being told that the important thing is whether I have made good men feel unfairly criticized, or whether someone believes my body permits me to speak as a woman. I can recognize nuance without surrendering the truth of my experiences. I can care about men who have suffered without minimizing harm done to women. I can welcome disagreement without accepting transphobia as a legitimate price of being heard.

So please, stop asking women to interrupt their pain with reassurance for people who did not cause it. Stop treating a transgender woman's identity as something that can be granted when she is agreeable and revoked when she is not. If you want to show that you are different, then show it in your conduct, in your boundaries, in your willingness to listen, and in what you do when someone else is being treated poorly. I was never trying to prove that all men cause harm, and I was never asking anyone to feel guilty simply for being a man. **I was asking people to take the harm seriously enough to help make it stop.**
